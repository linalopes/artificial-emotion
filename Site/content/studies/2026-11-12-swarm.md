## Question

What happens when a single mechanical movement becomes a collective behavior?

The development of [MauMau](/studies/maumau-first-parametric-enclosure/) began with a relatively simple mechanical task: raising and lowering a suspended object using a small motor, a spool and a line. SWARM takes that individual unit as a building block for a larger kinetic system.

Each module has only one axis of movement. The complexity I am interested in does not come from making the individual mechanism more elaborate, but from arranging nine of them in space and exploring the relationships between their movements. A change in timing, amplitude or phase can transform the character of the entire installation without changing its physical construction.

## Setup

### Three days of fabrication and assembly

In the days leading up to the Open Research Studio, I dedicated three days to turning the individual MauMau prototype into a ceiling-mounted installation. One day was spent laser-cutting the enclosures, another soldering the dedicated Artificial Emotion controller PCBs, and another assembling and installing the modules on the ceiling.

This was the first opportunity to bring the mechanical, electronic and computational work developed in the preceding studies together into a single installation.

### From a module to a matrix

SWARM consists of nine MauMau modules arranged in a fixed 3 × 3 matrix. Each raises and lowers a lightweight hollow glass sphere suspended on a line. The repeated elements provide a relatively consistent visual vocabulary, allowing their individual and collective movements to become the focus of the study.

| Element | Design decision |
| --- | --- |
| Spatial arrangement | Nine independently moving elements in a 3 × 3 ceiling-mounted matrix. |
| Actuation | One 28BYJ-48 geared stepper motor per module, winding and unwinding a line on a spool. |
| Electronics | One [Artificial Emotion Controller v1](/studies/artificial-emotion-controller-v1/) per module, using an ESP32-S3 SuperMini and a ULN2003A motor driver. |
| Reference position | A normally closed endstop provides a mechanical reference for homing each module. |
| Suspended objects | Lightweight hollow glass spheres, chosen as repeated elements for the movement study. |
| Control | Independent module identities, Mau01–Mau09, coordinated through a local Wi-Fi network. |

The decision to use one controller per motor extends the modularity beyond the mechanics. Each unit can be addressed independently, while the group can be treated as a single choreographic system.

The mechanical designs and electronics remain reusable outside SWARM. This installation is one possible arrangement of the MauMau framework, rather than the only object it was designed to produce.

### The digital interface as an instrument

The browser-based SWARM Control interface is built with Svelte, with a Node.js coordinator communicating with the individual ESP32 modules through WebSockets. The software distinguishes the composition of a movement score from its execution on physical hardware.

A movement score defines the intended trajectory of each module over time. The individual motor steps are generated on the modules themselves, rather than transmitted continuously by the browser.

The interface provides three initial choreographic families:

| Pattern | Movement principle |
| --- | --- |
| **Wave** | A periodic movement travels through the matrix, with phase offsets between modules. |
| **Breathing** | Expansion and contraction are suggested through coordinated vertical motion, with timing differences between the center and surrounding elements. |
| **Drift** | Individual trajectories follow slower variations, introducing less obviously regular collective behavior. |

Parameters such as amplitude, period, direction and phase offset make it possible to explore variations without rewriting the firmware or rebuilding the physical installation.

The distinction between **Preview** and **Live** is particularly important. Preview allows me to study a choreography computationally before sending it to the motors. Live connects the composition to the physical installation. The interface also provides individual module control, homing, trajectory timelines and system monitoring.

The digital representation is not simply a remote control. It gives me a way to study the behavior of the installation as a system: to inspect timing relationships, compare patterns, isolate individual movements and consider the choreography from different viewpoints. Planned trajectories and firmware-reported position estimates are different kinds of information; neither should be mistaken for an independent measurement of the physical position of a sphere.

## Observation

Seeing the nine modules in motion changes the scale of the experiment. A single sphere moving vertically is a simple mechanical event. Across a matrix, the same movement can suggest propagation, contraction, suspension or a shifting relationship between individual elements and the group.

I am particularly interested in how little needs to change mechanically for the perceptual character of the installation to change. The same nine objects can produce different compositions through changes in their temporal relationships alone.

The installation is also a useful physical research platform. Rather than developing a separate mechanism for every new movement idea, I can use the same structure to investigate different behaviors. The interface makes these variations accessible, while the physical installation exposes the limits and qualities that a digital preview cannot fully represent.

At this stage, SWARM is a working prototype prepared in the context of the Open Research Studio, not a finished or long-duration-validated installation. Its value is in making collective motion available as a material for further research.

## Next

After the Open Research Studio, the next phase of research will focus on the behavior of the system over longer periods and on the relationship between the digital score and its physical execution.

- **Choreographic studies:** explore how amplitude, speed, direction and phase relationships change the perception of collective movement.
- **Repeatability:** compare the physical starting and ending positions after repeated movement cycles, investigating any accumulated displacement.
- **Endurance:** test the installation over progressively longer durations and observe the reliability of the motors, spools, lines and electronics.
- **Synchronization and autonomy:** investigate whether the modules can sustain a shared choreography independently of the computer, and how monitoring and control can be recovered when the network becomes available again.
- **Homing and safety:** refine position referencing, endstop behavior, fault handling and accessible means of stopping the installation.
- **Digital observation:** develop clearer ways of distinguishing the planned choreography, reported module states and actual physical behavior.

The intention is to develop SWARM as an instrument for studying collective kinetic behavior, where the movement itself can be continuously composed, observed and reconsidered.
