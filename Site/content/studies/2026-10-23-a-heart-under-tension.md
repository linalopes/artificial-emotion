## Question — What happens when a heart becomes a fold?

The previous day's [Miura-ori in Organza](/studies/miura-ori-organza-heat-setting/) experiment established that a lightweight textile could retain a folded geometry after heat-setting and still open and close. I wanted to take that observation a little further: could the folded surface become a mechanism in its own right, and could an image embroidered onto it acquire movement without being animated independently of the fabric?

Around the same time, Mariana Tamashiro shared [*Tropisme* by Chloé Bensahel](/references/tropisme-chloe-bensahel/), an ongoing artistic research project in Paris. In the prototype she shared, I was particularly interested in the distinction between the points that hold a textile in place and the points through which movement is introduced. The wooden frame makes that relationship visible. It is not simply a support for displaying the fabric; it is part of the mechanism.

My starting condition was different. Rather than beginning with an extended membrane and introducing deformation through traction, I was working with a textile that already had a compressed resting configuration. The movement I wanted to introduce was **expansion**.

## Setup — Embroidery before folding

For this second Miura-ori experiment, I replaced the organza with a sheer, nude-coloured tulle and made a slightly larger folded sample. Before folding the textile and heat-setting it in the oven, I embroidered the outline of an anatomical heart by hand, using red thread and an embroidery hoop.

My husband found this rather funny. Hadn't I already invented a machine for embroidery? Why was I doing it by hand?

Because I like making things by hand, too.

I enjoy the possibility of moving between these different ways of making. Building a machine that can embroider does not mean that I no longer want to embroider. In this experiment, the handwork came first, followed by the folded geometry and the mechanical structure.

I used the same paper-mold and heat-setting approach documented in [the organza study](/studies/miura-ori-organza-heat-setting/). The exact dimensions of this larger sample and the fibre composition of the tulle were not recorded here. Rather than repeat the oven procedure, I am keeping that earlier study as the reference for the process.

What changes when the embroidery is made before the folding is that the heart becomes subject to the geometry of the textile. It is not a rigid image attached to a moving object. Its red lines are carried by a transparent surface whose folds can conceal, overlap and reveal parts of the drawing as it moves.

## Setup — Two anchors, six moving points

I mounted the folded tulle inside a small red frame. The upper and lower attachment points remain fixed. They establish the vertical position of the piece, while the lateral attachment points are responsible for opening it.

The movement I am exploring in this particular sample is predominantly horizontal. I am not trying to pull the middle of the fabric into a different silhouette, but to expand the folded surface across its width and allow it to return to its compressed configuration.

Pulling only one point on each side would concentrate the force in the middle. Instead, I stitched three small pulling threads into each lateral side of the textile — upper, middle and lower — and brought the three threads together through a small ring. There is one ring on the left and one on the right, each connected to a main pulling line.

The resulting arrangement separates the points of anchorage from the points of actuation:

- Two fixed anchors, at the top and bottom of the textile.
- Three moving attachment points on each side, six in total.
- Two small rings that collect the lateral pulling threads.
- Two main pulling lines, one for each side.
- Four small metal V-groove pulleys and additional guides around the frame to redirect the lines.

The V-groove pulleys are the same kind I have been using in the MauMau kinetic module. The earlier [A Membrane Under Tension](/studies/a-membrane-under-tension/) study explored how reinforced attachment points and reconfigurable connections could help me work with flexible textiles. Here, the question moves from testing attachment points to distributing a coordinated movement across them.

The rings simplify the transmission of force, but they do not guarantee that all three points travel equally. The lengths and angles of the individual threads remain part of the experiment.

## Observation — A material that closes by itself

The first tests were entirely manual. By pulling the two main lines, I could open the folded textile. When I released them, the tulle returned towards its compressed configuration without needing a second set of cables to pull it back.

This is an important distinction for the mechanism. I only need to actively produce the opening movement. The folded material itself supplies the return movement.

The two videos in the gallery document this hand-operated stage. They are not demonstrations of a motorized system.

Compared with the organza from the previous experiment, the tulle has a more open structure and feels less resistant. I wonder whether the more continuous surface of the organza might make it better suited to retaining and transmitting the folds. That would need a comparative test rather than a conclusion based on these two samples alone.

But there is something I particularly like about the tulle. Its transparency allows the red embroidery, folds, empty space and mechanical threads to remain visible at the same time. The opening and closing begin to suggest something almost respiratory. The heart is not reproducing an anatomical heartbeat; the entire surface seems to breathe.

## Turning MauMau upside down

When I started thinking about motorizing the frame, I realized that I might not need to design a new actuator at all.

MauMau already contains a stepper motor, a custom spool and a guide pulley. Its original configuration was developed to produce a vertical movement through a single line. What if I simply turned the entire module upside down?

![MauMau mounted upside down on the frame](https://res.cloudinary.com/da1flkgyb/image/upload/v1795098686/IMG_6453.webp)

I attached the inverted module to the frame. The opening in the acrylic enclosure originally intended for the sliding tube can become the entry point for the two pulling lines. The existing guide pulley may help direct them towards the spool, while the pulleys around the frame transform the winding motion into horizontal traction on the two sides of the fabric.

The physical mounting is already in place. The two lines have not yet been connected to the spool, and the motorized opening and closing have not yet been tested.

This is a small but meaningful change in how I see MauMau. Until now, it has primarily been a module for a particular kind of vertical movement. Here, the same mechanical unit could become useful for a different movement simply by changing its orientation and the routing of the lines.

**Same motor. Same spool. Different movement.**

The modularity is not only in being able to reproduce the enclosure or the electronics. It is in discovering what other mechanisms the same assembly can drive.

## Next — From a manual gesture to a motorized cycle

For the first motorized test, I want to preserve the simplicity of the manual mechanism rather than introduce independent motors for each attachment point.

The proposal is to use the existing [MauMau spool](/studies/maumau-spool-first-parametric-prototype/), with its 15 mm core diameter, and wind both main lines in the same effective direction on the same spool. An approximate 180-degree rotation would take up 23.6 mm of line on each side, or about 47 mm of combined horizontal travel under ideal direct-traction conditions. This is a nominal cable displacement, not a measured expansion of the textile: the geometry of the branches, slack and the behaviour of the folds will influence the actual result.

The [Artificial Emotion Controller v1](/studies/artificial-emotion-controller-v1/) already provides the motor driver and a physical USER button. A dedicated sketch for this experiment could begin with the motor stopped and use successive single clicks to start clockwise movement, stop, start counterclockwise movement and stop again. Each movement would have an approximate 180-degree limit, initially at 2 RPM. If a movement finishes automatically, the next click would start in the opposite direction.

There is no need for automatic homing in this first supervised test. Without a position sensor, however, the angular limit is relative to the start of a movement, not an absolute safe limit for the fabric. I need to establish the correct winding direction, maintain tension during release, and avoid winding past the closed position or pulling against the textile's limits. I will test the routing by hand before connecting the lines to the motor.

The next questions are whether the two lines can share the existing spool without crossing or interfering with one another, whether the three points on each side open the fabric evenly, and whether repeated cycling changes the folds or embroidered attachment points.

## Update — 16 November 2026

Yesterday, during the [Open Research Studio](/events/open-research-studio/) at my atelier in Wetzikon-Zürich, I noticed how many people found this small piece enchanting.

I am still trying to understand why.

Perhaps it is the lightness of the tulle, or the way the frame allows the surrounding space to remain visible through the textile. Perhaps it is the scale of the piece, with its tiny metal pulleys and almost invisible lines. Perhaps it is the familiarity of the embroidered heart combined with a movement that does not quite correspond to how we expect a heart to move.

There is a tenderness in the combination of these elements that I did not necessarily anticipate when I was working out where to place the pulleys or how to distribute the pulling force.

What interests me about this response is that the mechanism remains visible. The viewer can encounter the frame, the thread, the folds and the small pieces of hardware without that necessarily diminishing the sense of enchantment.

I do not yet know which qualities of the piece are responsible for that response, and I do not want to decide too quickly. The encounter with visitors is another part of the research: a way of noticing something about an experiment that may not have been apparent while making it.

For Artificial Emotion, this is a question worth carrying forward. How much movement is necessary for a material to begin to suggest a living presence? And how much of that impression already resides in the material, its scale, its transparency and the way it is held?
