## In Wetzikon

**School of Tomorrow's AI** is based in **Wetzikon, in the Greater Zurich area**, at the historic **Schönau-Areal** on Schönaustrasse 13.

The School moved into the Schönau in October 2025. We arrived in a place that had already lived through very different forms of production: an early nineteenth-century textile factory, later an industrial site, and today a temporary ecosystem of ateliers, workshops, cultural spaces and small creative practices.

That history matters to the work being made here.

The Schönau is not simply the address of the School. It has gradually become part of the way we think about making, technology and material culture.

## A former spinning mill

The **Spinnerei Schönau** was built in 1823 by Friedrich Nagel and became one of the early large industrial complexes along the Aabach. According to local historical documentation, stones from the east tower of Wetzikon Castle were reused in the construction of the factory.

The building belonged to the period in which mechanised textile production transformed Wetzikon and the Zürcher Oberland. Water power, spinning, machinery and factory labour reshaped the landscape around the Aabach, making textile production central to the region's industrial history.

Industrial production at the Schönau continued in different forms for more than a century. The spinning machinery finally stopped in 1991. Today, the former factory is used as a place for studios, cultural activity, small businesses and creative experimentation.

There is something productive in working with contemporary technologies inside this history.

We develop experiments with artificial intelligence, sensors, motors, code and new forms of interaction in rooms shaped by an earlier technological revolution. The machines are different, but questions around automation, labour, materials, bodies and production have never completely disappeared.

Over time, this relationship with the building has started to contaminate the research itself.

## Why the School exists

I founded School of Tomorrow's AI as a space for **AI for Creative Practices**: a place where artists, designers, educators and other creative practitioners can encounter artificial intelligence through making rather than through software training alone.

The School treats AI as a material for creative practice.

That means working not only with models and prompts, but also with bodies, objects, sensors, electronics, physical spaces, fabrication and collective experimentation.

A recurring principle is that people do not need to begin as programmers. They need a reason to make something, enough technical access to start, and a context in which unfinished experiments are allowed to exist.

This has shaped workshops around creative coding, machine learning, physical computing, embodied interaction and speculative technologies, but it has also shaped a broader methodology: prototype early, make the question physical, observe what happens, document it, and iterate.

## From a school to a research environment

The first phase of School of Tomorrow's AI was strongly centred on workshops and learning experiences.

After a year in Wetzikon, the space is beginning to change.

The School is gradually becoming not only a place where creative practitioners learn with technology, but also a place where longer artistic and technological investigations can develop. The intention is to strengthen this research-led dimension and, in time, transition the initiative toward an **association-based structure** that can support artistic research, education, collaborations and public programmes in Switzerland.

That transition is still in progress.

What is already clear is that the School is becoming an environment where teaching and artistic research can feed one another rather than remain separate activities.

## Artificial Emotion

Around one year after arriving at the Schönau, **Artificial Emotion** began to take shape as one of the research projects developed from this environment.

School of Tomorrow's AI is the project's **main research base in Switzerland**.

This is where many of its mechanisms are assembled, tested, broken and rebuilt. It is where motors, spools, sensors, textiles, electronics, sketches and unfinished prototypes accumulate around the studio. It is also where the work can be opened to other people through workshops, conversations, open studios and public research formats.

The relationship is not only spatial.

The pedagogical ideas developed through the School also influence the methodology of *Artificial Emotion*.

The same habits return:

- make the question tangible;
- work through prototypes rather than only descriptions;
- move quickly between concept and material;
- use technical constraints as part of the creative process;
- document failures as well as functioning systems;
- let encounters with other people alter the direction of the work.

In that sense, *Artificial Emotion* is both an artwork developed at the School and a way of testing what the School itself can become: a place where creative technology, artistic research and learning share the same physical and conceptual infrastructure.

## The Schönau as part of the research

The textile history of the Schönau has become increasingly difficult to treat as neutral background.

A project concerned with movement, soft mechanisms, tension, textiles and machine behaviour is being developed inside a former spinning mill.

That coincidence is beginning to generate its own questions.

What kinds of machines once occupied these rooms?

What materials passed through them?

What forms of bodily labour were organised around those machines?

What remains physically in the building, in archives and in the memory of Wetzikon?

These questions are beginning to connect *Artificial Emotion* to a wider investigation of the Schönau itself — not as nostalgia for industrial heritage, but as a way of understanding how different technological systems leave traces in bodies, materials and places.
