# Tropisme — Weaving Movement, Memory and Botanical Response

**Artist:** [Chloé Bensahel](https://www.chloebensahel.com/)  
**Project:** *Tropisme* (2025–2026, research in progress)  
**Context:** PEPR O2R — Organic Robotics artist residency, [ISIR](https://www.isir.upmc.fr/), Paris  
**Research dialogue:** Gilles Bailly and Baptiste Caramiaux are named in the residency documentation; their participation is marked as *to be confirmed* in the February 2026 event programme.  
**Research thread:** Soft Mechanisms  
**Discovered through:** Mariana Tamashiro, who shared the reference with me.

## Why this project caught my attention

Mariana Tamashiro sent me a video of a textile prototype being pulled into changing shapes by motors distributed around a wooden frame. The mechanism is visible: a flexible, perforated fabric is held under tension while wires run between its attachment points and motorized units around the perimeter. The surface seems to gather, sink and shift as different forces act on it.

What interests me is not simply that fabric can be animated. It is the possibility of obtaining a complex, seemingly bodily movement from comparatively simple acts of pulling and releasing. There is no need to sculpt every resulting fold in advance: the textile's structure and the distribution of forces take part in making the gesture.

This is a **prototype observation**, not a technical specification. From the Instagram image alone I cannot establish the exact motor type, cable routing, sensor system, control algorithm or whether the sequence is interactive or preprogrammed.

## What is *Tropisme*?

A *tropism* is a directional growth response of a living organism, commonly a plant, to an environmental stimulus such as light or gravity. In *Tropisme*, Bensahel turns to this botanical vocabulary as a way of thinking about robotic textiles. Rather than making a fabric merely imitate a moving plant, the research asks how woven and embroidered soft robotic materials could establish a relationship between **plant movement, human gesture and material response**.

The project is part of the French **PEPR O2R** programme for organic robotics and is hosted at the Institut des Systèmes Intelligents et de Robotique (ISIR) in Paris. The residency description places textile craft in dialogue with soft robotics, human–machine interaction, machine learning, interspecies communication and empathy toward the botanical world. Its announced ambition is to explore textiles that react to human gestures and incorporate robotic or sensing technologies. These are research aims, not confirmed functions of the particular Instagram prototype.

The February 2026 programme for the artist residencies describes the work as an encounter between traditional textile knowledge and high-technology sensing, with Gilles Bailly and Baptiste Caramiaux listed as scientific interlocutors, subject to confirmation. The project is consequently best understood as an **ongoing art–science investigation**, rather than a finished artwork with a settled technical architecture.

## Who is Chloé Bensahel, and how did she arrive here?

Bensahel is a French-born artist working with weaving, textile installation, performance, language and interactive technology. She moved to the United States as a child, studied integrated design with an emphasis on textiles at Parsons in New York, and graduated in 2013. She subsequently returned to France to deepen her practical knowledge of textile making. That passage between conceptual design and hands-on fabrication remains central to her work.

Since 2019 she has explored interactive tapestries, often combining traditional techniques with conductive thread, light or sound. In *Words Weave Worlds* (2019), developed with support from Google Jacquard and Google Arts & Culture, the tapestry is more than a woven image: interaction becomes a way of activating narratives and sensations held in the fabric. Other works such as *Body Memory* (2021) continue that concern with the histories carried by bodies, garments and materials.

Her research makes an important conceptual move: textiles are not only supports on which stories can be illustrated. Their **construction itself** can encode information. The crossings, sequences, repetitions and decisions involved in making a textile may be understood as a language with its own structure.

This line of inquiry became especially explicit during her 2023–2024 MIT residency, and helps me read *Tropisme* as part of a longer artistic trajectory rather than an isolated robotic experiment.

## Weaving Memory into Textiles — the fabric as an information structure

This earlier body of research is, for me, just as compelling as *Tropisme*, although it approaches another question. At MIT, Bensahel worked with Zach Lieberman's **Future Sketches** group, including Vera van de Seyp, to investigate historical forms of computational memory and the possibility of weaving textiles with magnetic properties.

A crucial reference was **magnetic-core memory**: early computer memory made from tiny magnetic cores threaded by wires. The magnetic state of each core could represent information. The association between handwork and computation is literal rather than metaphorical; the physical construction of such memory required highly precise manual threading. Bensahel had encountered historical examples through research connected to the Smithsonian, and became interested in the neglected histories of craft and labour within computing.

At MIT she explored whether a textile could be magnetized and potentially change its tension or configuration. The published material describes this as research and experimentation; it should not be interpreted as proof that a finished autonomous, shape-shifting fabric was produced.

What I particularly love here is the notion that the fabric has something like an **accessible DNA** — not genetic material, of course, but a readable logic of construction. Its crossings and material decisions contain a code, and code can hold memory. This is my interpretation, not Bensahel's claim. Rather than seeing information as something added *onto* a textile by electronics, this perspective asks what information may already be present *within* its structure, and what new forms of storage or response become possible when the textile itself is treated as an information medium.

In this sense, *Weaving Memory into Textiles* is not primarily a soft-mechanism reference. It is a deeper conceptual companion to *Tropisme*: one investigation concerns **how textile structures hold information**, while the other asks **how they might move and respond**. Together they suggest a possible continuity between construction, memory, sensing and behaviour, without implying that the two projects share the same mechanism.

## Reading the visible mechanics of the *Tropisme* prototype

In the Instagram post, the frame appears to carry several rotary actuator units around a stretched, perforated textile panel. Tensioning lines connect the boundary or reinforced points of the material to the surrounding assembly. The observed movement suggests a **cable-driven deformation system**: motors alter line lengths or tensions, while the fabric distributes the resulting forces into folds, cavities and changing curvature.

That description is a visual hypothesis, not confirmed documentation of the implementation. It is also important not to confuse this prototype with the magnetically actuated textiles explored at MIT: they are separate research contexts, and there is no evidence here that the *Tropisme* demonstrator uses magnetic actuation.

Even without a full bill of materials or control description, the prototype makes several concrete research questions visible:

- Where do pulling forces enter the textile, and how do those attachment points affect tearing, friction and deformation?
- How do several actuators produce one coordinated material gesture rather than a collection of independent motions?
- How much of the movement is determined by the timing of the motors, and how much by the textile's elasticity, geometry and recovery?
- Could a simple manual-pull test reveal the surface's movement vocabulary before any electronic control is introduced?

## Why it matters to Soft Mechanisms and Artificial Emotion

For **Soft Mechanisms**, *Tropisme* is a contemporary example of research in progress where the mechanism does not end at the motor or the transmission. The material is part of the system. Fabric tension, its reinforced points, its flexibility and its resistance to movement all help determine the final gesture.

For **Artificial Emotion**, the question becomes expressive: **when does a material deformation begin to look like a response, a hesitation, a contraction or a breath?** The interesting movement may occur not at the motor shaft but across the fabric, where several modest actions combine into something less mechanically obvious.

I also want to retain the intellectual connection with *Weaving Memory into Textiles*. There is a provocative difference between *controlling* a material and *reading* the information already contained in its construction. Bensahel's practice gives me a way of thinking about both: fabric as an active mechanical body and fabric as an information-bearing structure.

This is why I want to follow the development of *Tropisme* rather than treat the current prototype as a finished answer. The project is still making its own history.
