## Why it matters

In the **Soft Mechanisms** research thread of *Artificial Emotion*, folding is not only a visual treatment. It can give a flexible surface preferred directions of motion: a fabric that expands, contracts, collapses and returns to a recognisable geometry.

These three references approach this possibility from different directions: a practical tutorial for making folded fabric with paper molds, a research paper relating origami geometry to wearable structures, and a textile artwork that demonstrates the expressive potential of hand pleating and heat-setting.

Together they form a useful context for my [Miura-ori organza experiment](https://github.com/linalopes/artificial-emotion/blob/main/Site/content/studies/2026-10-21-miura-ori-organza-heat-setting.md). They are not three examples of the exact same crease pattern: the Instructables example uses a V-grid, Nguyen discusses several origami geometries including Miura-ori variations, and NUNO's *Origami Pleats* is an origami-inspired pleated textile.

## 1. Paper Mold Origami Fabrics 2 — V Grid

**Type:** Practical making tutorial  
**Platform:** Instructables  
**Source:** [Paper Mold Origami Fabrics 2 — V Grid](https://www.instructables.com/Paper-Mold-Origami-Fabrics-2-V-Grid/)

This tutorial demonstrates a direct fabrication principle: prepare two folded paper molds, sandwich a synthetic fabric between them, refold and compress the assembly, and apply heat so that the fabric retains the folds.

The author selects **synthetic organza** for its transparency and relative stiffness. Heavier drawing paper (90 lb) is used to make molds capable of holding their shape and producing crisp creases.

The documented oven treatment is approximately **160 °C for 25 minutes**. This is a reported process parameter, not a universal recommendation for every fabric, paper, or oven. It also concerns a **V-grid**, not Miura-ori.

What interests me here is how uncomplicated the tooling can be. The paper is simultaneously the drawing, the forming tool, and the temporary structure that holds the textile while it acquires a crease memory. The end result is a textile that can be manipulated after the mold is removed.

## 2. Uyen Nguyen — *Folding Fabric: Fashion from Origami* (2020)

**Type:** Conference research paper  
**Author:** Uyen Nguyen  
**Publication:** *Proceedings of Bridges 2020: Mathematics, Art, Music, Architecture, Education, Culture*, pp. 93–102  
**Paper:** [Read the PDF](https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2020/bridges2020-93.pdf)  
**Archive entry:** [Bridges 2020](https://2020.bridgesmathart.org/regular/93.html)

Nguyen studies how origami ideas can be transferred from paper to garments, with particular attention to **flat-foldability, the memory of folds, structural stiffness, and the geometry of changing forms**. Her experiments include heat-set synthetic textiles and versions with semirigid panels attached to fabric facets.

Her heat-setting method uses two matching paper molds with fabric between them. The folded assembly is placed in an oven, with a tray of water underneath to introduce steam. For polyester, she reports a typical temperature of about **300 °F (approximately 149 °C)**, followed by cooling while the piece remains folded. She does not specify a single universal baking time in that description and explicitly describes damaging fabric when the temperature was too high.

A particularly useful distinction emerges between **retaining a crease** and **supporting a three-dimensional surface**. Heat-setting introduces fold memory, but larger facets may still droop under gravity. Smaller facets or added support can change that behaviour. Nguyen also discusses a chevron top that varies the widths of a Miura-ori-derived pattern according to the Fibonacci sequence.

For *Artificial Emotion*, this raises a question beyond whether organza can be pleated: what happens to the movement when the **cell size, stiffness and scale** change?

## 3. Reiko Sudō / NUNO — *Origami Pleats* (1997)

**Type:** Textile artwork / design reference  
**Year:** 1997  
**Design:** Reiko Sudō, with collaborators including Hiroko Suwa and Mizue Okada  
**Producer:** NUNO Corporation, Japan  
**Material / process:** 100% polyester organza; hand folding, heat-setting, and in some versions heat-transfer printing  
**Artist / studio source:** [NUNO — Origami Pleats](https://www.nuno.com/en/textile/origami-pleats/)  
**Museum documentation:** [LACMA collection](https://collections.lacma.org/object/133888) · [Cooper Hewitt](https://www.cooperhewitt.org/2019/03/06/origami-pleat/) · [Powerhouse Collection](https://collection.powerhouse.com.au/object/354375)

*Origami Pleats* is an especially compelling precedent because the pleat is both a manufacturing technique and the character of the finished work. The transparent textile is hand-folded into geometric angles and fixed with heat. When opened, the pleats produce a changing play of translucency, depth and volume; when released, certain scarf versions spring back toward their compact folded state.

The process involved several collaborators, not Reiko Sudō alone. Museum documentation credits **Mizue Okada** with developing the pleating technique. The LACMA object record names **Reiko Sudō, Hiroko Kobayashi, and Mizue Okada**, with NUNO and Takekura Co., Ltd.; NUNO's project page names **Reiko Sudo, Hiroko Suwa, and Mizue Okada**. Credits depend on the particular object and source.

The Cooper Hewitt account describes a paper mold that holds polyester in its folded state during heat treatment. Coloured transfer paper can be added to introduce gradations of colour while the form is being fixed.

What is compelling here is not simply the resemblance to folded paper. It is the combination of **transparency, physical memory and reversible-looking movement**. The textile can be compressed into a dense object and expanded into a larger, lighter surface.

## Connections to Soft Mechanisms

These references suggest three related but distinct questions:

1. **Fabrication:** Can a hand-folded paper mold reliably transfer a crease pattern to synthetic organza?
2. **Mechanics:** How does the size of a folded cell change the textile's ability to expand, contract, support itself, and respond to a small force?
3. **Expression:** How do transparency, repetition and folding memory change the appearance of movement?

The first small Miura-ori organza study has already produced a surface that takes the fold pattern and opens and closes. The next question is how to **actuate it**, and how the behaviour changes in a larger sample.

Hand folding remains an important part of this direction. Slight irregularities are not necessarily defects: they reveal the relationship between the maker, the temporary paper mold and the resulting textile. An SVG pattern offers a reproducible alternative without making digital scoring or automated folding a requirement.
