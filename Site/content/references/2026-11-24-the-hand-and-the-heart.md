## An experiment from 2024

While revisiting earlier experiments in the studio, I came across an artificial hand holding an anatomical heart that I had made for Halloween in 2024. It belonged to a small kinetic prototype combining physical computing, machine learning, improvised mechanical components and lightweight sculptural materials.

The experiment predates *Artificial Emotion*. I am bringing it into the research notebook as a reference from my own practice, rather than as a new study developed within the project.

## Artificial anatomy and lightweight materials

The hand was constructed around a wire armature, covered with black EVA foam clay. I had begun experimenting with a textile covering, thinking about how a similar construction could be used to make articulated figures for stop-motion animation. The textile layer remained unfinished, leaving part of the underlying material visible.

The anatomical heart was sculpted from the same type of foam clay. I particularly enjoy working with this material because it becomes remarkably light once dry. For small kinetic objects, this quality matters mechanically as much as it does sculpturally: the material allows a relatively delicate mechanism to move a three-dimensional form without carrying unnecessary weight.

I remember a friend joking about how familiar I seemed to be with the anatomy of a heart. At the time, it was simply part of a Halloween experiment. Looking at the object again, the combination of an artificial hand and an artificial anatomical heart has acquired a different resonance within my current research.

## From a human gesture to a mechanical gesture

The original mechanism used a micro servo, a tensioned thread and repurposed components acting as pulleys and guides, mounted on a perforated wooden board. The thread was attached to the middle finger of the hand holding the heart. By rotating in opposite directions, the servo acted through the thread to move the articulated structure.

The archival video from 2024 reveals another layer of the experiment. A webcam and a browser-based machine-learning interface recognised my left and right hands. The recognition of one hand or the other determined the direction of movement of the physical mechanism.

What interests me in this arrangement is the translation between different kinds of gesture: a bodily gesture is recognised computationally, converted into a motor command, and transmitted through a thread to an artificial body. The movement of the sculptural hand is not a direct anatomical imitation of my own hand; it is mediated by the logic of the interface and the physical properties of the mechanism.

## Revisiting the mechanism in 2026

On 24 November 2026, I reassembled the prototype using the modular construction system I am developing for *Artificial Emotion*, based on a 20 × 20 mm grid inspired by OpenStructures.

The main intervention was the supporting structure: a more robust frame, a modular perforated panel and a custom 3D-printed servo mount designed with the assistance of AI.

I deliberately retained the original servo, electronics, improvised pulleys and sculptural elements instead of migrating them to the current MauMau controller architecture. The purpose was not to bring an earlier experiment up to the latest technical standard, but to make it physically accessible again without erasing its original construction logic. The 2026 documentation records this mechanical reassembly; the 2024 video preserves the earlier machine-learning interaction.

## Why it matters to Artificial Emotion

Several concerns that now belong to the **Soft Mechanisms** and **Kinetic Studies** research threads were already present in this small experiment: cable-driven movement, delicate articulation, lightweight materials, the relationship between rigid and soft elements, and the translation of bodily gestures into mechanical behaviour.

The heart also provides a visual connection to the anatomical vocabulary of the current research, although the original experiment was not a heartbeat or biosignal study.

Reassembling the object makes these continuities visible without suggesting that the questions of *Artificial Emotion* had already been formulated in 2024. The current research has developed quickly, but its development draws on years of experiments with materials, mechanisms, computation and embodied interaction.

The modular grid makes it possible to bring an older object back into the studio's living archive without requiring it to become a new prototype. In this sense, the research infrastructure supports not only the construction of new experiments, but also the preservation and reinterpretation of earlier ones.

Sometimes an existing object becomes a reference for a question that only emerges years after it was made.
